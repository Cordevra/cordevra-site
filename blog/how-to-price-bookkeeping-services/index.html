How to price bookkeeping services: hourly, fixed fee or value pricing

The trade-offs between hourly, fixed and value pricing, a simple way to build a monthly fee, and how to handle scope creep and price increases.

November 6, 2026 · 5 min read

Pricing is the question bookkeeping firm owners ask each other most, and the one with the least satisfying answer: it depends. It depends on your market, your experience, the clients you serve and how efficiently you work. What does not depend on any of that is the method. A good method gives you a price you can explain, defend and adjust.

A note before we start: we are not going to quote "the going rate." Rates vary a lot by country, region, specialty, credentials and client size, and published averages tend to mix very different kinds of work. The best benchmark is what comparable firms in your own market charge for comparable scope, which you can learn from peers, local associations and the prospects who tell you what they paid before.

The three main models

Hourly billing

You track time and bill for it at an agreed rate. Hourly billing is easy to start with and feels fair when the scope is genuinely unknown, such as a cleanup of books nobody has touched in two years.

The downsides are well known. Clients cannot predict their bill, so they hesitate to ask questions. You are penalized for getting faster: every process improvement reduces your revenue. And time tracking, done honestly, is its own administrative job.

Use it for: cleanup and catch-up work, one-off projects with unclear scope, and the first month or two with a new client while you learn the books.

Fixed monthly fee

You agree on a scope and charge the same amount each month. This is the most common model for ongoing bookkeeping because it suits recurring work: the client knows what they pay, and you keep the benefit of working efficiently.

The risk is scope. A fixed fee priced on twenty transactions a month becomes unprofitable at two hundred. Fixed fees only work with a clear scope definition and a habit of reviewing it.

Use it for: monthly bookkeeping, payroll, accounts payable and receivable, and other recurring services.

Value pricing

You price based on the value of the outcome to the client rather than your effort. A business preparing for a loan, a sale or an investor round may value timely, reliable financials far more than a business that only needs them at tax time.

Value pricing can be very profitable, but it requires real conversations about what the client is trying to achieve, and it is hard to apply to commodity work. Most firms use it for advisory, forecasting and premium tiers rather than basic reconciliation.

Use it for: advisory, CFO-style work, and packages built around a specific business outcome.

Building a fixed monthly fee, step by step

Most firms land on fixed fees for the core service. Here is a simple way to build one.

  1. List what drives effort. Number of bank and card accounts, monthly transaction volume, number of employees on payroll, bills paid per month, invoices sent, classes or locations tracked, and how often you need the client's input.
  2. Estimate hours per month for a client of that shape, based on your own history. If you do not have history yet, track time carefully for your first clients; it is the most useful data you will ever collect.
  3. Apply your target hourly rate. This is not the rate you show the client; it is the rate your business needs to cover salaries, software, overhead and profit.
  4. Add a buffer for questions, month-end surprises and the extra work in the first months.
  5. Round into tiers. Clients understand three or four packages much better than a custom formula.

A worked example with made-up numbers. Suppose a client takes about five hours a month and your target rate is $X per hour. The base fee is 5X. Add a 20% buffer and the fee becomes 6X. If your packages are priced at 4X, 6X and 9X, this client fits the middle tier. Replace X with your own rate; the method matters more than the figure.

Packaging: tiers that make sense to clients

Good tiers are defined by things the client can see, not by your hours. Common dividing lines:

  • Volume: transaction counts or number of accounts.
  • Speed: financials by the 20th of the month versus by the 10th.
  • Services: payroll, bill pay, invoicing and collections, 1099 preparation.
  • Access: a monthly review call, quarterly planning, or direct access to a senior team member.

Offering accounts payable and receivable as separate services is often an easy way to grow revenue from existing clients, because those services save the owner time they can feel.

Cleanup and catch-up work

Price cleanup separately from the monthly fee, always. It is unpredictable, it is front-loaded, and if you bundle it into the first months of a fixed fee you will either lose money or anchor the client to an inflated monthly price. Many firms quote cleanup hourly with an estimate and a cap, or as a fixed project fee after reviewing the books.

Scope creep and how to handle it

Scope creep in bookkeeping is gradual: a new bank account, a second location, a few more employees. Nobody announces it. Protect yourself with three habits:

  1. Write the volume assumptions into the engagement letter.
  2. Review each client's actual volume at least once a year.
  3. When volume moves into a different tier, say so, and adjust the fee at the next renewal.

Most clients accept a price change that is tied to something they can see, like twice as many transactions. They push back on increases that look arbitrary.

Raising prices

Review prices once a year. Give clients written notice well ahead of the change, explain what is included, and send an updated engagement letter for signature. If a client is unprofitable at any reasonable price, it is kinder to both sides to help them find a better fit than to keep doing the work at a loss.

What to track to know your pricing works

  • Effective hourly rate per client: fee divided by actual hours.
  • Clients whose volume has outgrown their tier.
  • Time spent waiting on clients, which often hides inside "slow" clients.
  • How long it takes to collect payment after you invoice.

Pricing is not a decision you make once. It is a loop: set a fee, track the work, compare, adjust. Firms that run that loop every year rarely end up with clients they resent.
