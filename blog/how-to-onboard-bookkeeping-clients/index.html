How to onboard bookkeeping clients: a practical checklist

The order we follow for every new client, from the discovery call to the first monthly close, and what to collect at each step.

November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

The first sixty days with a new bookkeeping client decide most of what the next few years look like. A clean onboarding sets expectations, gets you the access and documents you need, and shows the client how working with your firm feels. A rushed one leaves you chasing logins in month three and arguing about scope in month six.

This is the checklist we use. It is written for a small firm taking on a typical small business client, but the order works for larger engagements too. Adapt the details; keep the sequence.

Before you say yes: the discovery call

Onboarding starts before the client signs. The discovery call is where you find out whether the client is a fit and what the work really involves. Ask about:

  • The state of the books. When were they last reconciled? Who has been doing the work? Is there a backlog?
  • Volume. How many bank and card accounts, roughly how many transactions a month, how many employees, how many bills paid each month.
  • Systems. Which accounting software, payroll provider, payment processors and point-of-sale tools are in use.
  • Deadlines. Loan covenants, investor reports, sales tax filings, or a tax preparer waiting on year-end numbers.
  • Expectations. How quickly they expect monthly financials, how they like to communicate, and who on their side makes decisions.

Write the answers down where your team can find them later. They become the basis for your price, your scope and your workflow.

Step 1: Send a structured intake form

Do not rely on call notes for the details. Send a short intake form that captures legal name, entity type, fiscal year end, key contacts, the list of accounts and the systems they use. A form gives you consistent answers across clients and spares the client from repeating themselves in five emails.

Keep the form short enough to finish in one sitting. Anything that needs a document (such as a prior-year tax return) should be a separate request, not a form field.

Step 2: Engagement letter and scope

Put the scope in writing before you touch the books. A good bookkeeping engagement letter covers:

  • The services included, such as monthly reconciliation, financial statements, payroll or accounts payable.
  • What is not included, especially tax preparation, cleanup of prior periods and advisory work if you price those separately.
  • Your turnaround time for monthly financials and what you need from the client to meet it.
  • The fee, how and when it is billed, and how scope changes are handled.
  • Each side's responsibilities, including the client's responsibility to provide complete and accurate information.

Get it signed electronically and store the signed copy with the client's record, not in someone's downloads folder. If you have not settled on a pricing model yet, our guide on how to price bookkeeping services walks through the options.

Step 3: Access and permissions

Request access in a single, clear list. Typical items:

  • Accountant or user access to the accounting file, with the right permission level.
  • Read-only access to bank and card accounts, or a clear process for getting statements each month.
  • Access to the payroll provider and any payment processors.
  • Access to the bill pay or expense tool, if you will run payables.

Never ask clients to email you passwords. Use the invitation features in each system so the client controls access and can revoke it later. Keep a simple access log per client: what you have, who granted it and when.

Step 4: Collect the opening documents

For the first month you need more than a normal monthly request. A typical opening list includes:

  1. Bank and credit card statements for every account, back to the last reconciled period.
  2. Loan statements and amortization schedules.
  3. The prior-year tax return and year-end financial statements.
  4. Payroll reports for the current year.
  5. A list of open invoices and unpaid bills.
  6. Fixed asset records, lease agreements and any other recurring contracts.

Send the list as individual requests with clear names, not as one long email. Clients finish a checklist faster than a paragraph, and you can see what is still missing at a glance. We cover this in more depth in how to collect documents from clients without chasing.

Step 5: Assess the books and agree on cleanup

Once you have access and statements, review the file before promising anything. Look at reconciliations, the balance sheet, uncategorized items, undeposited funds, and whether prior periods have been closed. Most new clients need some cleanup; the question is how much.

If the cleanup is significant, quote it separately from the monthly fee and agree on a start date for the monthly work. Mixing the two makes your first months unprofitable and makes it hard for the client to understand what they are paying for.

Step 6: Set up the recurring work

Before the first close, set up the client's recurring workflow: the steps your team runs each month, the deadlines, and the document requests that go out automatically. Assign a preparer and a reviewer. If the client has payroll, sales tax or quarterly filings, add those cycles too.

A template saves real time here. Our recurring workflow templates article includes a starting point for monthly bookkeeping, payroll and year-end.

Step 7: The kickoff conversation

A short kickoff call with the client is worth the time. Walk them through:

  • Where they will find requests from you and how to upload documents.
  • When to expect monthly financials and what you need from them each month, and by when.
  • How to ask questions and how quickly you respond.
  • How you will handle transaction questions: a short list each month is normal and expected.

Clients who know the rhythm from the start answer faster. Clients who do not will default to whatever channel they used with their last bookkeeper.

Step 8: Run the first close, then review the onboarding

The first monthly close is part of onboarding. Expect more questions than usual and allow extra time. Once it is done, take ten minutes to note what went wrong: a missing account, a slow approver, a document nobody knew existed. Fix the client's workflow and update your onboarding checklist so the next client benefits.

The checklist, in one place

  1. Discovery call: books, volume, systems, deadlines, expectations.
  2. Intake form sent and completed.
  3. Engagement letter with scope and fee signed.
  4. Access granted to accounting, banking, payroll and payables tools.
  5. Opening documents requested and received.
  6. Books assessed; cleanup quoted separately if needed.
  7. Recurring workflow, deadlines and assignments set up.
  8. Kickoff call held.
  9. First close completed and onboarding reviewed.

None of these steps is complicated. The hard part is doing them the same way for every client, which is exactly what a checklist is for.
